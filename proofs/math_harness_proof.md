Proof of the prime number theorem. Write log for the natural logarithm, and set θ(x)=∑_{p≤x} log p, ψ(x)=∑_{p^k≤x} log p=∑_{n≤x}Λ(n), π(x)=#{p≤x}, where Λ(n)=log p if n=p^k for a prime p and an integer k≥1, and Λ(n)=0 otherwise. Lemma 1, Chebyshev bounds. For all sufficiently large x, (log 2)/4 x ≤ θ(x) ≤ x log 4. Proof: the upper bound is immediate from ∏_{p≤n}p≤4^n: θ(x)=θ(⌊x⌋)≤log(4^{⌊x⌋})≤x log 4. For the lower bound let B_m=binomial(2m,m). Since the 2m+1 terms in (1+1)^{2m}=∑_j binomial(2m,j) sum to 4^m and B_m is the largest term, B_m≥4^m/(2m+1). By Legendre's formula v_p(B_m)=∑_{j≥1}(⌊2m/p^j⌋−2⌊m/p^j⌋); each summand is 0 or 1 and vanishes when p^j>2m, so v_p(B_m)≤⌊log_p(2m)⌋=v_p(lcm(1,…,2m)). Hence B_m divides lcm(1,…,2m)=exp(ψ(2m)), giving ψ(2m)≥2m log 2−log(2m+1). The higher prime powers contribute ψ(2m)−θ(2m)=∑_{k≥2}θ((2m)^{1/k}); the sum is empty once (2m)^{1/k}<2, and using θ(y)≤y log 4 it is at most log 4(√(2m)+(2m)^{1/3}log_2(2m))=O(√m). Thus θ(2m)≥2m log 2−O(√m), so for large m, θ(2m)≥((log 2)/2)(2m). For arbitrary large x choose m=⌊x/2⌋; then 2m≥x/2 and θ(x)≥θ(2m)≥((log 2)/4)x. Lemma 2, partial summation and equivalences. Riemann-Stieltjes integration by parts gives, for x≥2, θ(x)=π(x)log x−∫_2^x π(t)dt/t, and inverting the same formula gives π(x)=θ(x)/log x+∫_2^x θ(t)/(t log^2 t)dt. If θ(x)∼x, Lemma 1 gives θ(t)=O(t), so the integral is O(∫_2^x dt/log^2 t)=O(x/log^2 x)=o(x/log x), while θ(x)/log x∼x/log x; hence π(x)∼x/log x. Conversely, if π(x)∼x/log x, then π(t)=O(t/log t), so ∫_2^xπ(t)dt/t=O(x/log x)=o(x), and θ(x)=π(x)log x−o(x)=x+o(x); hence θ(x)∼x. Also ψ(x)=∑_{1≤k≤log_2 x}θ(x^{1/k}), so ψ(x)−θ(x)=θ(x^{1/2})+∑_{k=3}^{⌊log_2 x⌋}θ(x^{1/k})=O(x^{1/2}+x^{1/3}log x)=O(x^{Therefore θ(x)∼x implies π(x)∼x/log x and ψ(x)∼x. Section 2, Euler product and logarithmic derivative. For σ=Re{s}∞. For σ=Re{s}>1, ∑_n n^{-σ}<∞ and ∑_n(log n)n^{-σ}<∞, so the Dirichlet series gives ζ(s)=∑_{n≥1}n^{-s}, and termwise differentiation gives ζ'(s)=−∑_{n≥1}(log n)n^{-s}. For finite x, E_x(s)=∏_{p≤x}(1−p^{-s})^{-1}=∑_{n:P(n)≤x}n^{-s}, where P(n) denotes all prime factors of n. If n is omitted then n>x, so the tail is at most ∑_{n>x}n^{-σ}, which tends to 0; the same estimate for the differentiated sums gives E_x'(s)→ζ'(s). Since ∑_p p^{-σ}<∞, the infinite Euler product converges absolutely and to a nonzero value, namely ζ(s). For each finite x, E_x'(s)/E_x(s)=−∑_{p≤x}(log p)p^{-s}/(1−p^{-s}); letting x→∞ yields −ζ'(s)/ζ(s)=∑_p(log p)p^{-s}/(1−p^{-s}). The double series ∑_{p,k≥1}(log p)p^{-kσ} is dominated by ∑_{n≥2}(log n)n^{-σ}<∞, so the geometric series may be rearranged: −ζ'(s)/ζ(s)=∑_{p}∑_{k≥1}(log p)(p^k)^{-s}=∑_{n≥1}Λ(n)n^{-s}. Section 3, Mellin form. For Re{s}>1, Tonelli's theorem and ∫_n^∞ t^{-s−1}dt=n^{-s}/s give ∑_{n≥1}Λ(n)n^{-s}=s∫_1^∞∑_{n≥1}Λ(n)1_{n≤t}t^{-s−1}dt=s∫_1^∞ψ(t)t^{-s−1}dt. The same transform sends the model function ψ(t)=t to s∫_1^∞t^{-s}dt=s/(s−1)=1+1/(s−1), a simple pole with residue 1, matching the asymptotic ψ(t)∼t. Section 4, continuation and residue of ζ. Using the right-continuous floor function, ζ(s)=∫_{1^-}^∞u^{-s}d⌊u⌋=s∫_1^∞⌊u⌋u^{-s−1}du for Re{s}>1. Dividing by s and splitting ⌊u⌋=u+(⌊u⌋−u) gives ζ(s)/s=1/(s−1)+∫_1^∞(⌊u⌋−u)u^{-s−1}du. Because |⌊u⌋−u|≤1 and ∫_1^∞u^{-σ0−1}du<∞ whenever σ0>0, the remainder integral converges locally uniformly and holomorphically for Re{s}>0. Hence by the identity theorem ζ(s)/s, and therefore ζ(s), continues to Re{s}>0; equivalently ζ(s)=s/(s−1)+s∫_1^∞(⌊u⌋−u)u^{-s−1}du. Near s=1 this is ζ(s)=1+1/(s−1)+O(s−1), so ζ has exactly one pole in Re{s}>0, a simple pole at s=1 of residue 1. Put G(s)=(s−1)ζ(s); then G is holomorphic near the closed half-plane Re{s}≥1 and G(1)=1. Section 5, no zeros on Re{s}=1. For t=0, s=1 is the pole, not a zero. Fix t≠0 and suppose ζ(1+it)=0 of order m≥1. For real σ>1 define Φ_t(σ)=ζ(σ)^3ζ(σ+it)^4ζ(σ+2it). Using −ζ'/ζ(s)=∑_{n≥1}Λ(n)n^{-s}, the logarithmic derivative is (d/dσ)log|Φ_t(σ)|=−∑_{n≥2}Λ(n)n^{-σ}(3+4cos(t log n)+cos(2t log n)). Since 3+4cosθ+cos2θ=2(1+cosθ)^2≥0 and Λ(n)≥0, this derivative is ≤0; as σ→∞ all three zeta factors tend to 1, so |Φ_t(σ)|≥1 for every σ>1. On the other hand, near s=1, ζ(s)=1/(s−1)+O(1), ζ(s+it)=c(s−1)^m+O((s−1)^{m+1}) with c≠0, and ζ(s+2it) is finite because 1+2it≠1. Hence Φ_t(s)=O((s−1)^{4m−3}), and since m≥1, |Φ_t(σ)|→0 as σ↓1, contradiction. Thus ζ(1+it)≠0 for every real t. Together with the nonzero Euler product for Re{s}>1, ζ has no zeros on Re{s}≥1. Section 6, boundary behavior of −ζ'/ζ. Since G(s)=(s−1)ζ(s) is zero-free on Re{s}≥1 and G(1)=1, each point of that closed half-plane has a small zero-free disk; hence H(s)=−G'(s)/G(s) is holomorphic on an open set containing Re{s}≥1. For s≠1, logarithmic differentiation of ζ(s)=G(s)/(s−1) gives ζ'/ζ(s)=G'(s)/G(s)−1/(s−1), so −ζ'/ζ(s)=1/(s−1)+H(s). Therefore −ζ'/ζ is holomorphic in a neighborhood of {Re{s}≥1}\{1} and continuous up to Re{s}=1 away from s=1; moreover (s−1)(−ζ'/ζ(s))=1+(s−1)H(s)→1 as s→1. Thus −ζ'/ζ has on Re{s}≥1 only a simple pole at s=1, with residue R=1. Section 7, the nonnegative Wiener-Ikehara theorem. Theorem: if a_n≥0, A(x)=∑_{n≤x}a_n, F(s)=∑_{n≥1}a_n n^{-s}=s∫_1^∞A(t)t^{-s−1}dt for Re{s}>1, and F(s)−R/(s−1) extends continuously to Re{s}≥1, then A(x)∼Rx. Proof of the reduction: put x=e^y and q(y)=e^{-y}A(e^y). Nonnegativity makes A nondecreasing, and q(y+h)≥e^{-h}q(y) for h≥0, so q is relatively slowly decreasing. Its Laplace transform is L(λ)=∫_0^∞e^{-λy}q(y)dy=∫_1^∞A(x)x^{-λ−2}dx=F(1+λ)/(1+λ) for Re{λ}>0. If M(s)=F(s)−R/(s−1), the hypothesis says M extends continuously to Re{s}≥1, and then L(λ)−R/λ=−R/(1+λ)+M(1+λ)/(1+λ) also extends continuously to Re{λ}≥0. The one-sided Wiener-Hardy-Littlewood-Karamata lemma states that a nonnegative relatively slowly decreasing locally bounded-variation function whose Laplace transform differs from R/λ by a function continuous on Re{λ}≥0 must satisfy q(y)→R; this is the standard nonnegative Tauberian input, proved by Newman's contour argument for the inverse Laplace transform, with relative slow decrease supplying exactly the one-sided condition. Applying the lemma gives q(y)→R, and with x=e^y, A(x)/x=q(log x)→R. Section 8, assembly. Take a_n=Λ(n), A(x)=ψ(x). By Section 2, F(s)=∑Λ(n)n^{-s}=−ζ'/ζ(s) for Re{s}>1; by Section 3 this equals s∫_1^∞ψ(t)t^{-s−1}dt. Sections 4 through 6 show F(s)−1/(s−1)=H(s) is holomorphic on Re{s}>1 and extends continuously to Re{s}≥1, with only a simple pole at s=1 of residue R=1. Since Λ(n)≥0, the theorem in Section 7 applies and yields ψ(x)∼x. Section 2 then gives θ(x)=ψ(x)+O(x^{1/2}log x)=x+o(x), so θ(x)∼x, and finally π(x)=θ(x)/log x+∫_2^xθ(t)/(t log^2 t)dt∼x/log x+o(x/log x). Hence π(x)∼x/log x, the prime number theorem.
